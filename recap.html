Whole plant · ~1 m

The brief we set ourselves

Design a monocot root system with the best soil exploration capacity we can manage. Everything that follows is a constraint on that design problem — and the root already has five jobs to do before we start optimising anything.

01Water uptakeBulk flow to the xylem, driven by transpiration
02Nutrient uptakeSelective, against a gradient, and expensive
03AnchorageMechanical work against lodging and impedance
04StorageCarbon and reserves banked below ground
05SensingGravity, water, touch, neighbours, microbes

And the side effects we do not usually put on the list: roots feed the soil microbiome, deposit carbon into the soil profile, and open the channels that let water infiltrate.

Two lenses on the same organ

Morphology is the study of form and how that form develops. Anatomy — from Greek ana- ("apart") + tomē ("to cut") — is what you see once you cut it open. The lecture used both, and they answer different questions.

Soil profile · 0–60 cm

The two nutrients live at different depths

Phosphate barely moves: it sorbs to soil particles and stays where it was deposited, so the P pool is a shallow one. Nitrate moves with the water and leaches down. One root system cannot be optimal for both — which is why architecture is a trade-off, not a maximum.

Trait list from the lecture

Topsoil foraging improves P capture

Shallow basal root growth angles, more adventitious (crown) roots, more basal root whorls, dispersed laterals, longer and denser root hairs — plus aerenchyma and root etiolation to make all that length cheaper to run.

    Apex · 0–2 mm

    The cap: a sacrificial nose that also steers

    The cap is doing three jobs at once — lubricating its way through soil, feeding and defending the meristem behind it, and reading the direction of gravity. Tilt the root and watch the statoliths fall.

    What the cap is made of
    • Mucilagea polysaccharide gel that cuts friction and lets the tip penetrate
    • Columellacentral files holding dense, starch-filled amyloplasts — the statoliths
    • Border cellsreleased alive; their secretome traps and neutralises pathogens before they reach the meristem (Hawes et al. 2003; Wen et al. 2009)

    The sign trap: in roots, auxin accumulating on the lower flank inhibits elongation. The upper flank keeps growing, so the tip curves down. In shoots the same signal does the opposite.

    The framing to keep

    The root is a polarized epithelium (Barberon & Geldner 2014). Like the epithelium lining your gut, it has an outside face and an inside face, and it puts different transporters on each. That polarity is what turns a tube of cells into a selective tube of cells — and it only works if the space between the cells is sealed. Hold that thought.

    Radial file · ≈250 µm

    Three ways in — and one of them is a hole in the argument

    Follow one file of cells from the soil to the xylem. Water and everything dissolved in it has three routes across. Press play, then look hard at the yellow one.

    Route
    • Apoplastthrough the cell-wall continuum. Never crosses a membrane, so nothing selects what travels. Fast, cheap, indiscriminate.
    • Symplastone membrane crossing at the epidermis, then cell to cell through plasmodesmata. Selective at the front door.
    • Transcellularin and out across a membrane at every cell, largely through aquaporins. Most control, most cost.
    The problem on the screen

    A root that only had the apoplast would be a straw

    It would deliver soil solution — sodium, aluminium, heavy metals, whatever is dissolved out there — straight into the xylem and up to the leaves, unexamined. The polarity of the epithelium would be pointless, because everything could simply walk around the cells.

    So the root does the only thing that fixes it: it glues the wall shut — all the way around the endodermis, and in maize around the exodermis as well. Press the red button.

    The seal itself · ≈0.5 µm

    Lecture 2 starts here

    Robert Caspary described this band in 1865 and, in a monograph that is otherwise pure anatomy, allowed himself exactly one sentence of speculation about what it might be for.

    "That such a greatly thickened, poreless cell layer that is joined together without extracellular spaces, and which surrounds the body of the vascular bundle as a tube, is especially difficult to permeate for gases and liquids, can be assumed."

    Robert Caspary, 1865 — translation by the Geldner lab

    He was right, and it took another 150 years to work out how the cell builds it, how it knows where to put it, and what happens to a plant that cannot. That is where we pick up.

    How is it built, and how does the cell know where?

    CASP proteins, the CSD membrane domain, and the ESB1/peroxidase machinery that polymerises lignin in one band — Zhang et al. 2024.

    What does a mutant look like?

    The red-dye leak test. Wild type holds the dye out of the stele; the mutant does not. A leak test, exactly as you would pressure-test a pipe.

    Why does calcium care?

    Ca²⁺ moves almost entirely apoplastically and almost entirely in the xylem — so the strip, and transpiration, set where it ends up. Blossom-end rot is the failure mode.

    Why is the endodermis not the only barrier?

    Maize and most grasses build a second Casparian band in the exodermis, just under the epidermis — so the apoplast is stopped twice, at the way in and at the way to the stele.

    What does all this cost?

    Cortical cell size, aerenchyma, cell wall width — the metabolic-cost argument, LAT imaging, and RootSlice simulation.

    How to still have this in a year

    A fort, its walls, and its gates

    A root is working a crowded marketplace. It has to take what it wants, leave what it does not, and not get robbed. Its solution is radial deployment: rings of tissue like the walls of a fort, with gates in the wall — and the gates are the only way through.

    • Capthe scout — reads gravity, clears the path, takes the damage
    • Cortexthe bulk of the fort. Cheap volume is the whole design problem
    • Endodermisthe wall. Next lecture: the mortar that makes it a wall
    • Stelethe keep — once you are in, you ride the xylem to the shoot
